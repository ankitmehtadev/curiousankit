If you work on the corporate side of a company, you must have heard these buzzwords. While they are widely used in organisations, not everyone understands the uses, benefits, requirements and differences of these complex and expensive systems. I will try to cover these aspects in this post.

<Level n={1} title="Business Intelligence (BI)" />

Managers are the most important decision makers in an organisation, and they have an in depth knowledge of the entire business operation. Business Intelligence (BI) systems, often called Decision Support Systems (DSS), give managers a platform to make, visualise, analyse and improve important business decisions. These complex decisions might be based on different subjects of information (for example sales, customers or employees) collected by different departments of the organisation and stored in a common place called a data warehouse.

Whenever required, BI systems pull information from this data warehouse, apply complex analytical algorithms to it, and present it to managers as reports, with charts and tables that show the right information effectively and efficiently. These reports help managers make strategic decisions and plan the organisation's next move.

BI systems do not replace a manager's judgement, but they support the decision making process, and their benefits are often qualitative rather than quantitative. Business Intelligence gives managers the ability to consider more alternatives for a decision, a broader understanding of the business, a better and faster approach to problems, and the means to fine tune business plans.

<Level n={2} title="Supply Chain Management (SCM)" />

This is a complicated system covering an entire chain of activities: acquiring raw materials from vendors and suppliers, manufacturing the actual product, packaging and distributing it to wholesalers and retailers, and finally supplying it to the end consumer. A supply chain management (SCM) system integrates and controls the flow of information between all the departments of the organisation (including marketing, sales, finance and manufacturing) and makes sure the entire supply chain runs smoothly.

SCM gives a clear understanding of the entire manufacturing process. It also helps plan and automate certain stages of the supply chain, so products are made and delivered faster and more efficiently to the right consumer. Developing and maintaining SCM involves high complexity and costs. Because of its complex nature, crucial functions are often overlooked, and companies risk errors in their results.

<Level n={3} title="Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP)" label="Deeper still" short="Level 3" />

Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP) systems are used to manage and integrate an organisation's business processes, including sales and marketing, manufacturing, human resources and product planning. An ERP acts as a one stop application to manage and automate all the resources of an entire enterprise, from technology and services to people. It is the enterprise's source of information.

That is why large enterprises are usually the ones using these systems. Smaller businesses use lightweight versions of ERP to come up with business plans and solutions. The risks of implementing an ERP include cultural issues within the organisation, the cost of training employees to use it, choosing the right consultant, and keeping top management involved throughout the implementation.

<Level n={4} title="Customer Relationship Management (CRM)" label="Even deeper" short="Level 4" />

Customer Relationship Management (CRM) is a process that enables companies to collect customer data, understand customer preferences, run marketing campaigns, and deliver sales, customer service and support strategies that meet customers' needs. It helps companies reach the right customers at the right time, and analyse the results of those strategies using all the customer data collected. To get the most out of a CRM system, service delivery and response times need to go hand in hand with it.

<Level n={5} title="How the four fit together" />

All four of these systems are extremely costly to develop and maintain, so there is a high risk of companies losing time and money on these projects. These complex systems collect, analyse and use large amounts of data, so a dedicated team of technical and analytical experts is needed to customise and run them.

However, an organisation can use all four systems to generate large profits by improving its efficiency and growing effectively. For example, decision support tools can identify and assess the many factors that affect the performance of a sustainable supply chain. The use of ERP systems has grown in every industry. Almost all large companies use ERP solutions from leading vendors like SAP and Oracle. Many supply chain management vendors provide supply chain planning functions that rely on ERP systems to carry out the planned tasks. Meanwhile, many CRM systems are now integrated with ERP solutions, or come as part of them.
