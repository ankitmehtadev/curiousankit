"Congratulations, son! I am very proud of you! Now that you have started earning, invest some money into an index fund every month. This will help secure your future," my father told me on a phone call the day I received my first paycheck in January 2015. My response: "Yeah Dad, I'll do it eventually. By the way, how's the weather there?" My parents lived in a different city, and I was obviously trying to avoid the conversation. After all, I had just become independent! I made my own money. I was a grown up now! The first thing I did with my paycheck? Bought the latest amazing smartphone. My father, on the other hand, started investing a small amount from his salary every month on my behalf, into an index fund and a fixed income bond, with me as the primary beneficiary. This was on top of the investments he had already been making on my behalf since I was a kid. Meanwhile, I completely forgot about it, enjoying my newfound freedom.

Obviously, I wasn't grown up enough!

Fast forward to May 2020. By now I was living in Melbourne, Australia, one of the world's most liveable cities, and the one with the longest COVID lockdowns. Companies were losing money, people were losing jobs, and governments were panicking and making random decisions. No one was prepared for this.

However, those who still had a job (I was among the lucky ones) enjoyed the newfound comfort of working from home.

One Sunday morning, with nowhere to go and not much to do while locked down at home, I decided to finally explore the world of money. My first two Google searches (yep, ChatGPT didn't exist back then):

1. "What is money?"
2. "What is the history of money?"

What I found blew my mind. The deeper I went into the topic, the more fascinating, and funnier, it got.

Ever since then, I've picked up lessons from all sorts of places: books, podcasts, historical events, inspiring people, and more than a few mistakes of my own. I started scribbling these lessons down in a notebook, just for myself.

But the more people I met, the more I realised that many are in the same boat as me, making the same mistakes.

So I decided to start sharing my notes.

Some things you might agree with. Some you might not. That's perfectly fine. I truly value different perspectives, and if something I've shared sparks a new thought, or even a disagreement, I'd love to hear about it. Your experience might just teach me something new.

I am neither a "finance guru" nor a "crypto bro". I am not a super successful millionaire… yet. I can't claim to know everything about finance, and I don't enjoy following people who claim to have cracked money and make most of their living selling half baked courses to confused and underconfident people, with a promise to make them instantly rich. But I do respect them, because they taught me something too. More on that in my third lesson below.

<Level n={1} title="Understand the difference between money and wealth" />

You get your shiny new credit card with a credit limit of $10,000. You open your bank's app and check the balance. Do you see that $10,000 as money you can spend, or as a $10,000 debt?

The answer to this question will clarify your understanding of money versus wealth.

Money is earned. Wealth is built. Money keeps you going. Wealth sets you free. Money is what you pay in exchange for goods and services. Wealth is what remains even after the money stops flowing. Money is a tool. Wealth is the foundation for security and freedom, and a way to generate future money. Put simply, the money that remains after spending on all the necessary and unnecessary things in life is your wealth.

<PullQuote>Spend money while protecting your wealth.</PullQuote>

<Level n={2} title="Never depend on a single source of income. Diversify." />

We live in a world where unpredictability is the only constant. Wars, pandemics, natural disasters, impulsive politicians making rash decisions, cringey content creators getting rich by fearmongering, AI taking over jobs in organisations, and countries divided on immigration all add to this unpredictability.

When the COVID-19 pandemic hit Australia, many companies reduced the pay and work days of their staff to stay afloat. Both my work days and my pay in my full time role were affected. I worked 4 days a week instead of 5, which meant a 20% pay cut. I had no option but to accept the change so I could pay the bills. I don't blame the company. It was clearly a tough decision they had to make.

Luckily, since 2018 I had also been working as a teaching assistant at my alma mater in the evenings, alongside my full time job. This meant I worked non stop for 12 hours a day, two days a week.

Was it worth it? Keep reading…

Even though I had taken a 20% pay cut with no guarantee about the future, I still had a second stream of income. With one day off from my main job, I immediately took on extra sessions and subjects that semester and the next.

Fast forward to today: I work at a bigger company, in a much more senior position, and take home more than four times the salary I earned at that company. And I still teach two sessions a week in the evenings at the university.

If you are skilled at something, turn it into a product or service others are willing to pay for.

<PullQuote>Security and freedom come from having options.</PullQuote>

**Bonus tip:** don't have multiple sources of income that need the same core skill set or belong to the same industry. For example, don't run two businesses with the same target audience, or that are affected by the same market cycles.

<Level n={3} title="Get skilled at earning money passively, or with minimal daily effort" label="Deeper still" short="Level 3" />

Learn how to invest your money properly, and automate it. That could mean investing in well known index funds, individual company stocks, commodities such as gold and silver, bonds, startups and businesses. Spend some time understanding how the world works: basic macroeconomics. And of course, feel free to dive into microeconomics if it intrigues you.

Basically, either learn how to invest in something that will earn you money passively, or invest in developing a skill set that lets you build and earn from a product or service with minimal daily effort. This is why I said earlier that I respect those finance course sellers.

Imagine yourself working 28,404,000 hours a month to earn money.

Have I gone mad? Nope. Let me explain.

Apple, one of the largest companies in the world, has approximately 164,000 employees (as of 2024), working 8 hours a day, 5 days a week, around the world.

Once you invest in Apple stock, those 164,000 employees are working over 28 million hours a month combined to make sure the value of your shares goes up and you make a profit. All of this while you live your normal life during the day and sleep peacefully at night.

Now do you think I have gone mad? I guess not.

Tools like index funds make it even easier to invest and earn passive income based on your risk appetite.

I am not saying you should become the Wolf of Wall Street, nor am I claiming to be one. If stocks aren't for you, build the skills to create great quality courses or digital products which, once set up, can give you a steady, passive flow of income without much time spent managing them.

<Level n={4} title="Avoid debt" label="Even deeper" short="Level 4" />

You might ask me, "Hey smarty pants, what about a home loan?"

I agree there are debts you can't escape, such as a home loan or an education loan. But even with these, you must think very carefully about what is a sustainable and practical amount for you to pay off. Just because the bank gives you a borrowing capacity of $2 million doesn't mean you should borrow $2 million. It is not free money. You pay interest every month from your hard earned income. So think carefully. Maybe you only need to borrow $800,000 at a manageable interest rate, with the aim of systematically paying it off and becoming debt free as early as possible.

I know there are finance geniuses out there saying debt is great, and that you should leverage the bank's money instead of using your own wealth for spending. I agree with them to a certain extent. But many of these "finzards" (finance wizards… I'm a dad, so I have the right to crack such jokes, and yes, I'm patting myself on the back for this one) fail to explain that you should only do it if you have enough assets to settle that debt on any given day.

Simply put, if you have $10,000 saved up, then feel free to borrow $5,000 from the bank and use it for spending. The $10,000 you have saved can be invested to earn a profit. Now, if on any given day you need to pay back the debt for whatever reason, you can use that $10,000 to repay it, including any interest you might have incurred.

This way, all you do is win, win, win, no matter what!

Yes, I just quoted DJ Khaled.

<Level n={5} title="Spend less than you earn" />

Isn't it obvious? Yes, it is. But it is important to keep reminding yourself, because it is very easy to lose track. Especially if you are addicted to swiping (or tapping) your credit card everywhere.

I use a credit card, not because I love debt or because I can't afford to pay. There are 5 reasons I use one:

1. **Points.** I use them for discounted or free flights, or for discounts on everyday household items and groceries.
2. **Security.** If my card details get stolen (it has happened to me twice) and the thief goes on a shopping spree, I can immediately block the card, contact the bank and flag the fraudulent transactions. A new card is issued and all the money the thief spent is credited back. No questions asked.
3. **Free travel insurance** on domestic and international trips.
4. **A positive credit rating.**
5. **Savings on my home loan.** By keeping my money in my offset account and using my credit card for daily transactions, I save on the interest charged on my home loan, which is calculated daily. I clear my credit card balance the day I get paid.

The main thing here is to pay off your credit card balance in full and on time. To do that, it is extremely important to keep track of your daily spending and never go beyond your monthly budget.

So I recommend getting a credit card only if you understand the difference between money and wealth (see Level 1 if you skipped it).

Now do I finally sound like a grown up to you?

I hope so. My father will be very proud.
